# Rethinking Compact (<1M) Deep Learning Models for Brain Tumor MRI Diagnosis: Efficiency, Generalization, and Uncertainty-Gated Inference

## Abstract

Deep learning has transformed medical image analysis, becoming central to many clinical applications. However, real-world deployment remains challenging due to computational demands, latency constraints, and privacy concerns associated with cloud-based processing. Despite these limitations, state-of-the-art models increasingly favor larger architectures and datasets, reinforcing a scale-driven paradigm that is often impractical for resource-constrained systems.

This study systematically evaluates deep learning models with fewer than one million parameters on small-to-medium-sized datasets for image classification and detection, focusing on efficiency and cross-dataset generalization. Results show that on saturated classification benchmarks, compact models can achieve performance statistically comparable to models an order of magnitude larger. The best model attains 98.3% accuracy at 0.36 GFLOPs, running six times faster with less than a one-percentage-point accuracy difference. However, a performance gap remains on more challenging cross-dataset tasks, with errors concentrated in low-confidence predictions sensitive to input perturbations. The most efficient architecture, which uses parallel atrous convolution branches to capture multi-scale features, is particularly vulnerable to scale-sensitive inputs.

To address this limitation, we propose **MCG-TTA**, an architecture-informed inference strategy designed to improve robustness while limiting computational overhead. MCG-TTA improves slice-level F1 by up to 1.8 percentage points at approximately 1.9× the baseline latency, while retaining a model size of 0.91 million parameters. These findings suggest that, for resource-constrained clinical applications, uncertainty-aware inference can recover residual accuracy more efficiently than increasing model complexity.

## 1. Introduction

Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) have established foundational breakthroughs across core computer vision tasks, ranging from large-scale visual recognition [11, 24] to dense spatial reasoning and object detection [14, 53]. This trajectory was catalyzed by Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) [23], which embedded translation equivariance and local spatial hierarchies into learnable architectures. In recent years, the computer vision landscape has been profoundly shaped by the advent of Vision Transformers (ViTs) [9], masked image modeling [46], and self-supervised foundation backbones [1]. However, this contemporary trajectory has predominantly reinforced a "scale-driven" paradigm, wherein marginal performance increments are achieved by training networks with tens or hundreds of millions—and increasingly billions—of parameters over colossal compute clusters and proprietary web-scale datasets [11, 42].

In parallel, deep learning has revolutionized medical image analysis [10, 27], demonstrating specialist-level efficacy in complex diagnostic tasks [10]. Within neuro-oncology, non-invasive Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) serves as the primary clinical imaging modality for the detection, differential diagnosis, and histologic grading of intracranial neoplasms, including gliomas, meningiomas, and pituitary adenomas [2, 34]. Deep learning architectures applied to multi-sequence brain MRI have automated diagnostic workflows that historically required exhaustive manual radiological review [2, 34, 38]. Standard clinical diagnostic paradigms rely heavily on overparameterized deep backbones—such as deep ResNets [14], VGG networks [39], and compound-scaled EfficientNets [40]—to identify subtle textural heterogeneity, necrotic cores, and invasive margins across disparate axial, coronal, and sagittal planes [36, 44].

Despite these empirical successes, a critical translational divide persists between benchtop benchmark accuracy and clinical, point-of-care utility [20, 26]. In healthcare environments, real-world deployment rarely occurs in centralized, high-throughput cloud data centers. Routine transmission of high-resolution volumetric neuroimaging to external cloud infrastructures encounters severe regulatory barriers, notably patient confidentiality mandates and data protection governance such as HIPAA in the United States and GDPR in the European Union [10, 26]. Furthermore, cloud dependency introduces vulnerability to variable network bandwidth, transmission latency, and service outages—risks that are unacceptable during acute clinical scenarios, emergency neuro-triage, or intra-operative surgical navigation [20, 33]. Consequently, bringing intelligence directly to the clinical edge—operating locally on hospital workstations, embedded scanner consoles, and mobile intra-operative carts—has emerged as an urgent imperative [12, 25, 26]. 

However, edge deployment imposes uncompromising physical constraints: strict inference latency windows, severely constrained Random Access Memory (RAM), low thermal design power (TDP), and limited on-chip arithmetic hardware [12, 33, 41]. To accommodate mobile and decentralized environments, the vision community introduced lightweight architectural designs, including depthwise separable convolutions in MobileNets [15, 37] and Xception [17], channel shuffling in ShuffleNet [32], and cheap linear feature generation in GhostNet [13]. Yet, the overwhelming majority of these "efficient" models predominantly inhabit a mobile-scale budget of 2 to 20 million parameters. In contrast, the sub-1M parameter regime (<1,000,000 parameters) remains fundamentally underexplored in medical imaging, particularly under the practical constraints of small-to-medium institutional datasets [3, 21]. Crucially, as observed in recent foundational studies on compact architectures [21], naively downscaling standard architectures via uniform width or depth multipliers leads to erratic representational capacity, gradient instability, and disproportionate performance collapse [21, 24].

This investigation is motivated by the absence of systematic benchmarks, architectural insights, and deployment guidelines for deep learning models operating under a strict sub-1M parameter ceiling in neuro-oncological imaging. We posit that as the parameter budget is aggressively constrained, architectural topology—specifically how parameters are structurally allocated to capture multi-scale context without parameter redundancy—matters far more than sheer parameter volume [21]. While multi-path atrous (dilated) depthwise convolutions have emerged as a premier mechanism for expanding effective receptive fields without parameter inflation [5, 21], their behavior, domain robustness, and failure mechanics under clinical distribution shifts have never been systematically characterized.

To address these foundational questions, this work presents a systematic empirical evaluation of deep learning models operating under a strict sub-1M parameter budget across representative neuro-oncological imaging tasks. Our empirical findings uncover two fundamental phenomena:
1. **In-Domain Saturation**: On canonical diagnostic benchmarks, architecturally optimized compact models match the diagnostic capability of overparameterized backbones an order of magnitude larger, while offering substantially faster inference speeds on unaccelerated edge processors. This demonstrates that for constrained clinical cohorts, high parameter capacity often yields diminishing returns.
2. **Cross-Dataset Multi-Scale Vulnerability**: When evaluated under out-of-domain clinical shifts, this saturation breaks. We find that compact models relying on parallel atrous convolutions become brittle under input scale perturbations, with predictive uncertainty concentrating along ambiguous lesion boundaries.

In resource-constrained healthcare environments, resorting to larger models or heavy ensemble backbones to mitigate domain fragility is practically impermissible due to strict latency and memory limits [21, 41]. Instead, we show that this residual accuracy gap can be recovered far more efficiently at inference time by leveraging the model's own architectural inductive biases. To this end, we introduce **Multi-Scale Consistency Gated Test-Time Augmentation (MCG-TTA)**. Rather than performing costly, unconditional multi-view inference across every image [4, 47], MCG-TTA monitors prediction uncertainty and selectively triggers targeted scale augmentations that align with the network's internal receptive field structure only when scale disagreement is detected, requiring zero additional learned parameters.

Extensive evaluations confirm that uncertainty-aware test-time calibration stabilizes compact representations under distribution shifts while preserving patient-level diagnostic reliability. These results demonstrate that architecture-informed inference calibration offers a practical, hardware-friendly alternative to model scaling for clinical edge artificial intelligence.

In summary, the principal contributions of this work are:
- **Systematic Sub-1M Benchmark and Edge Profiling**: We provide a comprehensive evaluation of sub-1M parameter vision architectures on neuro-oncological imaging, analyzing trade-offs across model capacity, real-device edge CPU latency, throughput, and cross-cohort generalization against mobile and overparameterized baselines, complemented by patient-level clinical sensitivity analysis.
- **Characterization of Saturation and Domain Vulnerabilities**: We identify the saturation behavior of compact networks on in-domain medical diagnosis, and formally uncover the scale-sensitivity mechanics of compact atrous convolutions under clinical distribution shifts.
- **Architecture-Informed Test-Time Inference**: We introduce MCG-TTA, a parameter-free, uncertainty-gated inference framework that dynamically stabilizes scale-sensitive compact representations at test time with minimal latency overhead.
